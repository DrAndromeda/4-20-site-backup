Strain names are among the most visible features of cannabis culture, and among the least scientific. This page explains what the term is meant to describe, why it fails in practice and what gives a more reliable picture of a product.

## What a strain is

**A strain, or cultivar, is a named line of plants propagated for particular traits.** In agriculture, a cultivar is kept stable through cuttings or controlled seed breeding. In cannabis, the word "strain" is used loosely: it can refer to a single clone, to a seed line or just to a marketing label. Breeders cross plants to select traits such as yield, flowering time, resin production and aroma, and names often reflect lineage or branding rather than a verified identity.

## Why names are unreliable

**The same name on two products does not guarantee the same chemistry.** Reasons include:

- There is no central registry or naming authority; anyone can use a popular name.
- Seeds from the same named line segregate, so offspring vary.
- Growing conditions, harvest time, drying and storage change cannabinoid and terpene levels.
- Mislabelling and relabelling occur in unregulated markets.

Genetic studies that compared commercially labelled cultivars have found considerable overlap and inconsistency between names, which supports a cautious attitude toward names.

## Chemovars and chemotypes

**Chemovar describes a plant by its chemistry, not its name.** A chemotype is the characteristic profile of cannabinoids and terpenes. A broad classification used in research sorts plants by THC-to-CBD ratio: THC-dominant, mixed or CBD-dominant, plus rare types dominated by other cannabinoids. Terpenes, such as [myrcene](/en/terpenes/myrcene/) and [limonene](/en/terpenes/limonene/), add a second layer of description.

## What lab testing adds

**A certificate of analysis (COA) tells you what is in a particular batch.** A good COA comes from an independent accredited laboratory and states the batch or lot number and test date. It usually reports THC and CBD content (often as the acid forms THCA and CBDA plus the decarboxylated forms), may report terpene content, and may include screening for pesticides, heavy metals, residual solvents and microbial contamination. Potency numbers help people judge dose, which matters for safety as explained in [dosing and safety](/en/consumption/dosing-and-safety/). A report that is old, unlabelled or from the seller's own lab deserves less trust.

## The indica and sativa label

**Indica and sativa originally described plant shape and origin, and they are poor predictors of effect.** Botanically, the labels referred to plant morphology and regions of origin. Folk wisdom says indica relaxes and sativa energises, but studies comparing the chemistry and genetics of commercially labelled products have not found that these labels reliably separate chemical profiles. Many products are hybrids of uncertain ancestry. Personal response is also shaped by dose, route, tolerance and setting, so a label cannot tell you how a given person will feel.

## Risks and who should be careful

Higher potency raises the risk of anxiety, rapid heartbeat and impaired judgement, regardless of strain name. Anyone with a history of psychosis, adolescents, and pregnant people should avoid cannabis. Do not drive after use.
