Myrcene is one of the most frequently reported terpenes in laboratory analyses of cannabis flower. It is a small, volatile hydrocarbon that gives many plants a musky, earthy and slightly fruity scent, and it is a good example of how a single aroma molecule can be both widely familiar and poorly understood from a medical point of view.

## What myrcene is

**Myrcene is an acyclic monoterpene built from two isoprene units.** Its formula is C₁₀H₁₆ and its molar mass is about 136.2 g/mol. Chemists distinguish beta-myrcene, the form most often discussed in plants, from the less common alpha isomer. Like all terpenes, it is produced in the plant's glandular trichomes, the same resin glands that make cannabinoids.

Because it is a lightweight molecule, myrcene evaporates easily. This is why a freshly ground flower smells stronger than a jar that has been left open for weeks: much of the volatile fraction is lost to air.

## Where it occurs

**Myrcene is not unique to cannabis; it is common across the plant kingdom.** Hops are the classic source, and the fresh, resinous character of hoppy beer owes part of its aroma to myrcene. It is also found in mango, lemongrass, thyme, bay leaves and parsley. In essential-oil and food industries it is used as a flavour and fragrance component.

In cannabis, myrcene is often the dominant terpene in the profile, but not always. The terpene content of a cultivar depends on genetics, growing conditions, harvest time, drying and storage. Total terpene content in dried flower is commonly a few percent of weight at most, and the share taken by myrcene can vary widely from one batch to another, even within the same named strain. For that reason the label on a jar is a weak guide, as the page on [strains](/en/strains/) explains.

## Physical properties and heat

**Myrcene boils at roughly 167 °C, which is lower than THC and most other cannabinoids.** That matters in two ways. First, terpenes begin to evaporate at temperatures well below those needed to decarboxylate THCA, so heating plant material drives off part of the aroma. Second, the low boiling point is one reason why aroma and flavour change noticeably with the temperature of a heating device.

Myrcene is also oxidised slowly by air and light, and it can polymerise or convert into other compounds during long storage. Cool, dark and airtight storage slows this process, which is simply good practice for preserving any volatile plant material.

## What the evidence says

**Evidence for myrcene's biological effects comes mostly from cell and animal experiments, not from human trials.** Laboratory studies have reported anti-inflammatory and analgesic-like activity of myrcene in rodents, and some papers describe sedative-like effects at high doses in animals. These findings are interesting but they cannot be translated directly to people: doses used in animals are often far higher than anything a person would inhale from cannabis, and rodent behaviour is an imperfect model for human experience.

There are no large, well-controlled human trials showing that myrcene on its own treats any condition. A related and popular idea is the "entourage effect", the proposal that terpenes and cannabinoids modify each other's actions. It is a legitimate hypothesis, yet the human data are limited and mixed, and it should not be presented as settled.

## The mango folklore

**The idea that eating a mango before cannabis intensifies the effect has no solid scientific support.** Mango does contain myrcene, but the amount in a fruit is small, it is eaten rather than inhaled, and it is absorbed and metabolised differently from the myrcene present in cannabis smoke or vapour. No well-designed human study has demonstrated the claim. It persists as an anecdote, and individual impressions are easily explained by expectation, setting and the dose of THC itself.

The two molecules are isomers, sharing the same formula but arranged differently, which is why their smells are so different. See the page on [limonene](/en/terpenes/limonene/) for the citrus counterpart.

## Safety and practical notes

**Myrcene as a food flavouring is not considered a concern at typical dietary levels, but that says little about inhaling concentrated products.** Inhaled terpenes can irritate the airways, especially at high concentrations or when heated strongly. People with asthma or other lung conditions may be more sensitive to vapour and smoke of any kind.

Terpene-related claims should never replace medical advice. Anyone who is pregnant, who takes sedatives, or who has a heart or lung condition should talk to a physician before using cannabis products, and nobody should drive after consuming cannabis. For the cannabinoid side of the story, read about [THC](/en/cannabinoids/thc/) and [CBD](/en/cannabinoids/cbd/).

## Risks and who should be careful

- Concentrated terpene products can irritate the throat and lungs.
- Terpenes do not make a product safer or less intoxicating; the THC content still determines most of the risk.
- Marketing that attributes specific effects to myrcene is not backed by robust human data.
- Adolescents, pregnant people and anyone with psychosis risk should avoid cannabis products altogether.

Terpene research is moving quickly, and conclusions may change as controlled studies appear. Until then, the honest summary is that myrcene is an abundant, aromatic and interesting molecule whose effects in people remain largely unproven.
