Limonene is probably the terpene most people have already met without knowing it: it is what you smell when you scratch the peel of a lemon or an orange. The same molecule turns up in some cannabis cultivars, where it contributes a citrus note to the overall aroma. Beyond the scent, however, much of what is said about limonene online goes further than the science does.

## What limonene is

**Limonene is a cyclic monoterpene, a ten-carbon molecule built from two isoprene units.** Its formula is C₁₀H₁₆ and its molar mass is about 136.2 g/mol. It exists in two mirror-image forms. D-limonene, the form dominant in citrus peel, smells of oranges, while l-limonene has a more piney, turpentine-like scent. Most references to limonene in cannabis mean the d-form.

Limonene is a nonpolar, volatile liquid that dissolves in oils and alcohols but hardly in water. It is the reason citrus-based cleaning products and solvents work on grease.

## Where it occurs

**Citrus peel oils are the richest natural source, and limonene often makes up the large majority of orange peel essential oil.** Lemons, limes and grapefruit also contain it, as do juniper, peppermint, dill and some other herbs. In cannabis, limonene is usually present in smaller amounts than in citrus oil and is only one of several terpenes. Some cultivars are described as lemony or citrusy in laboratory terpene reports, but the proportion varies from batch to batch.

As with other terpenes, the content in dried flower is affected by genetics, cultivation, harvest, drying and storage, so the terpene numbers on one lab report do not guarantee the same profile next time. See [strains](/en/strains/) for why names are an unreliable guide.

## Physical properties and heat

**Limonene boils at roughly 176 °C, a little higher than myrcene, which boils near 167 °C.** Terpenes with lower boiling points evaporate first, so the aroma of heated plant material shifts as temperature rises. Limonene is also flammable, with a relatively low flash point, which is a practical safety point for anyone handling concentrated citrus oils or terpene-rich extracts near heat or flame.

When exposed to air, limonene oxidises to products such as limonene hydroperoxides. These are the compounds mainly responsible for skin sensitisation reported with aged citrus oils. Fresh, properly stored material is less of a concern than old, oxidised material.

## What the evidence says

**The research on limonene is largely preclinical, meaning cell cultures and animals, and results cannot be assumed to apply to people.** Laboratory and animal studies have explored anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anxiolytic-like and anticancer-related activity. Such work is useful for generating hypotheses, but the doses, routes of administration and species differ greatly from a person using cannabis.

Some small human studies have looked at citrus aromas or limonene supplements in relation to mood or digestion, and results are limited and not consistent enough to support firm conclusions. No regulator has approved limonene as a treatment for any condition. Claims that a limonene-rich cannabis cultivar will lift mood, relieve stress or fight disease are not supported by solid human evidence.

## Safety and everyday use

**Limonene is widely used as a flavouring and in household products and is generally regarded as low in toxicity at those levels, but concentrated exposure can cause problems.** Undiluted citrus oils can irritate the skin and eyes, and oxidised limonene can trigger allergic contact dermatitis in sensitised people. Inhaling concentrated terpenes in vapour can irritate the airways, particularly in people with asthma. Ingesting concentrated essential oils is not advisable, and they should be kept out of the reach of children and pets.

Limonene does not change the intoxicating potential of THC in a way that makes a product safer. Anyone using cannabis should keep in mind the usual precautions covered in [dosing and safety](/en/consumption/dosing-and-safety/), and should not drive afterwards.

## Risks and who should be careful

- People with asthma or sensitive airways may react to terpene-rich vapour or smoke.
- People with known fragrance or citrus-oil allergies can react to concentrated limonene.
- Pregnant people, adolescents and people with a history of psychosis should avoid cannabis products.
- Do not treat limonene content as a promise of any mood or health effect.

In short, limonene is a well-characterised, pleasant-smelling molecule with a long history in food and fragrance. Its role in cannabis is real as an aroma component, while its therapeutic reputation is still a matter of early research. For related chemistry, read about [THC](/en/cannabinoids/thc/).
