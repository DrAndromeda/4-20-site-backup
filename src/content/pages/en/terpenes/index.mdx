Terpenes are the reason a cannabis flower smells of citrus, pine, pepper or lavender rather than only of "cannabis". They are also among the most over-interpreted compounds in the field. This page explains what terpenes are chemically, what they do for the plant, how the major ones differ and where the evidence about effects in people stands.

## What terpenes are

Terpenes are hydrocarbons built from five-carbon isoprene units. Monoterpenes contain two units (10 carbons), sesquiterpenes three (15 carbons), and diterpenes four (20 carbons). Terpenoids is the broader term for terpenes that have been modified, for example with an oxygen atom, such as linalool or bisabolol. In cannabis writing, the two words are used interchangeably.

Reviews describe more than 200 volatile compounds in cannabis, of which monoterpenes usually dominate the fresh plant and sesquiterpenes are more persistent after drying. Total terpene content in dried flower is typically around 1 to 3 percent by weight, and rarely higher, although this varies a great deal by cultivar, growing conditions and handling. In comparison, cannabinoids in drug-type flower often reach 15 to 25 percent.

Cannabis does not make terpenes that no other plant produces. What is distinctive is the particular combination, together with the cannabinoids that occur in the same trichomes.

## Why the plant makes them

Terpenes are made in the same glandular trichomes as cannabinoids, and evidence from plant biology suggests they serve defensive and ecological functions. Many terpenes deter herbivores and insects, are toxic to some fungi and bacteria, and attract pollinators or predators of pests. The sticky resin also protects flowers from drying and UV radiation. Terpenes and cannabinoids share a precursor, geranyl pyrophosphate. None of this means the compounds are evolved for human benefit.

## Volatility and boiling points

Terpenes are volatile, which is the property that makes them smellable and also easy to lose. Many monoterpenes boil at lower temperatures than the cannabinoids, so they evaporate during drying, curing and vaporising.

Approximate boiling points at atmospheric pressure, according to chemistry references, are about 155 degrees Celsius for alpha-pinene, 166 for beta-pinene, 167 for myrcene, 176 for limonene, 186 for terpinolene and 198 for linalool. Sesquiterpenes such as beta-caryophyllene (roughly 260) and bisabolol (above 300) boil considerably higher, which is one reason they persist longer. Figures vary slightly between sources and are for orientation only. Lower-temperature vaporising preserves more terpenes, but a lower temperature also releases less cannabinoid.

## How terpenes are made

Terpene biosynthesis begins with two five-carbon building blocks, isopentenyl pyrophosphate and dimethylallyl pyrophosphate, which in plant trichomes come mainly from the plastid-based MEP pathway. Prenyltransferase enzymes join them into geranyl pyrophosphate (10 carbons) and farnesyl pyrophosphate (15 carbons). Terpene synthase enzymes then fold these precursors into specific monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes. The cannabis genome contains a sizeable family of terpene synthase genes, and different cultivars express different combinations, which is why chemical profiles differ between plants.

Environment matters too. Light, temperature, nutrient supply, plant age and harvest timing all influence terpene levels, so the same genetics can give different results in different growing conditions.

## Testing and labelling

Terpene numbers on a label are only as reliable as the laboratory method behind them. Terpene analysis usually uses gas chromatography, and sample handling can change results because the compounds evaporate during grinding and preparation. Different laboratories may report different totals for the same material, and standards for terpene testing are less developed than those for cannabinoids. Where terpene percentages are printed on a product, treat them as approximate. Claims that tie a named terpene to a specific health outcome on a product label are generally not backed by clinical evidence, and regulators such as the FDA have taken action against unproven health claims for cannabis-derived products.

## What the evidence says about effects

For most terpenes, the evidence is preclinical, meaning it comes from cells and animals, and the doses used in these experiments are often much higher than those a person gets from cannabis. A review by Ethan Russo (British Journal of Pharmacology, 2011) is widely cited for proposing that terpenes may modulate cannabinoid effects. It is a hypothesis-generating review and not clinical proof.

Here is what can reasonably be said:

- **Beta-caryophyllene** binds to and activates CB2 receptors, according to laboratory work published since 2008. This is a real, documented mechanism, and it has been studied in animal models of inflammation and pain. Because CB2 is not the main route for intoxication, caryophyllene is not expected to cause a high. Human clinical data for cannabis-relevant doses are limited.
- **Limonene and linalool** have anxiety-related effects in some animal studies and small aromatherapy studies. Whether inhaled cannabis levels do the same is unknown.
- **Pinene** has been reported to inhibit acetylcholinesterase in vitro, which led to speculation about counteracting THC-related memory effects. This is speculative and not shown in people with cannabis.
- **Myrcene** has analgesic and muscle-relaxant effects in some rodent studies at high doses. Human evidence is lacking.

Controlled studies that compare cannabis with the same THC content and different terpene profiles remain few. NCCIH and other bodies do not currently recommend selecting a product by terpene profile for medical purposes. ## Myths: indica, sativa and myrcene

The common claim that myrcene-rich "indica" causes couch-lock and limonene-rich "sativa" energises is not supported by good evidence. It rests on three weak links. First, the indica and sativa labels do not reliably track plant genetics or chemistry (see [cannabis explained](/en/about/)). Second, myrcene levels do not cleanly separate the groups in chemical surveys. Third, there are no well-controlled human trials showing that myrcene sedates at the amounts found in cannabis. The idea also traces to a popular but unverified claim that mangoes, which contain myrcene, enhance cannabis effects, which has no reliable data.

Aroma and effect are also easily confused. A product that smells piney and bright may feel more stimulating partly because of expectation. Set and setting, dose, tolerance, the THC to CBD ratio and the route of intake all affect the experience, and typically more than terpene content does.

## Storage and loss of terpenes

Terpenes are lost to evaporation, oxidation and heat, and the losses begin the moment the plant is harvested. Drying and curing can remove a large share of the monoterpenes, and flower stored badly can lose most of its aroma within months.

General principles from post-harvest research on aromatic plants:

- keep material cool, away from direct light and in airtight containers;
- avoid repeated opening, which refreshes oxygen and promotes evaporation;
- expect oxidation to alter terpenes, creating less pleasant, hay-like or sharp smells;
- be aware that heat above their boiling points, or burning, destroys terpenes, and combustion creates other compounds you do not want to inhale.

Terpene-containing products also raise safety questions. Concentrated terpenes are irritants, and not all terpenes have been studied for inhalation safety. Several can trigger allergic reactions or skin and airway irritation in sensitive people. Natural does not mean harmless.

## Risks and who should be careful

Terpenes found in food are generally recognised as safe at dietary levels, but inhaling concentrated terpenes or taking isolated terpene products is a different matter. People with asthma may react to strong aromas. Pregnant or breastfeeding women, people taking medicines and anyone with a medical condition should check with a doctor, because many terpenes are processed by liver enzymes.
