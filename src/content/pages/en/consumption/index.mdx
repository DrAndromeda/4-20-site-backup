This page explains how different routes of intake work and what risks each carries. It is educational and harm-reduction oriented; it does not encourage use. Cannabis is illegal or restricted in many places (see the [law overview](/en/law/)), and anyone with a medical question should talk to a clinician.

## Why the route matters

The way cannabinoids enter the body decides how quickly effects start, how long they last and how predictable they are. Inhalation delivers THC to the blood through the lungs within minutes, whereas swallowing sends it through the gut and liver first, producing a delayed, longer and often stronger effect at a given dose.

All figures on this page are broad ranges. Individual differences in body weight, metabolism, tolerance, stomach contents and the product itself can shift onset and duration considerably, and published studies use different dosing and measurement methods. Treat the numbers as orientation, not precision.

The compounds involved include [THC](/en/cannabinoids/thc/), which is intoxicating, and [CBD](/en/cannabinoids/cbd/), which is not. Products differ widely in the ratio and total amount of each.

## Inhalation: smoking and vaping

Inhaled cannabis acts fastest: effects usually begin within minutes, peak within roughly 10 to 30 minutes and fade over about 2 to 3 hours, although subtle impairment may last longer. Bioavailability is often quoted in a broad range of roughly 10 to 35 percent, because the amount absorbed varies with depth of inhalation, breath holding and losses in smoke.

Smoking involves combustion. Cannabis smoke contains tar and many of the same irritants and toxic combustion products found in tobacco smoke, and regular smoking is associated with cough, phlegm and bronchitis symptoms. Mixing cannabis with tobacco adds nicotine dependence and the full risks of tobacco. Whether cannabis smoking alone causes lung cancer is less clear than for tobacco, and evidence is mixed.

Vaporising heats plant material or concentrate below the point of combustion, which may reduce some smoke-related exposure. Concentrates and extracts, however, can contain very high THC percentages, which raises the risk of strong intoxication and dependence. In 2019 a lung-injury outbreak known as EVALI in the United States was linked mainly to vitamin E acetate in illicit THC vape cartridges, a reminder that unregulated products carry additional risks.

## Oral route: edibles and oils

Swallowed cannabis is absorbed slowly and unevenly. Onset typically takes 30 to 120 minutes, effects peak at about 2 to 4 hours and can last 4 to 8 hours or more, with after-effects such as drowsiness lingering. Oral bioavailability of THC is low and variable, often described as around 5 to 20 percent, because much is lost in first-pass metabolism.

The liver converts THC into 11-hydroxy-THC, which crosses into the brain readily and is considered at least as potent as THC. This is the main reason edibles can feel stronger and more disorienting than smoking the same amount.

The most common harm is accidental overdose through impatience: a person feels nothing after 30 minutes, takes more, and then experiences the full effect of both doses. Other recurring problems are inconsistent dosing in home-made food, products that look like sweets and are eaten by children, and confusion about labels. Emergency visits by young children who swallowed edibles are a documented concern in places where such products are sold.

## Sublingual and oromucosal products

Tinctures and sprays held under the tongue or in the cheek are absorbed through the oral mucosa, which partly bypasses the gut and first-pass metabolism. Onset is commonly described as 15 to 45 minutes, with duration of about 4 to 6 hours. In practice some of the dose is also swallowed, so the profile lies between inhalation and edibles.

Oromucosal sprays containing THC and CBD in a fixed ratio exist as licensed medicines in some countries for specific conditions, which shows that route and dose can be standardised under medical supervision. Over-the-counter oils and tinctures have no such guarantee, and published analyses of unregulated CBD products have found mislabelled content in some cases.

## Topicals and transdermal products

Creams, balms and oils applied to the skin act mainly locally. Most cannabinoids do not cross the skin barrier in large amounts, so ordinary topicals are not expected to cause intoxication. Transdermal patches are designed for deeper delivery and can produce measurable blood levels, but they are rare and mostly experimental. Evidence for benefit from topical products is limited, and skin allergies or irritation are possible.

## Potency, dose and tolerance

Potency has risen over recent decades, and higher THC content makes dose control harder. EUDA monitoring in Europe has reported rising average THC in herbal cannabis and resin over time, and concentrates can contain many times more THC than dried flower. A higher percentage does not necessarily mean a person will use less, so the total amount of THC taken is what matters.

Tolerance develops with frequent use: the same dose produces a weaker effect, and a person may take more to compensate. Tolerance fades after a break, so someone who has stopped for weeks can react more strongly to a former dose. Sensitivity also varies widely between people because of genetics, body composition and previous exposure. The same product can therefore feel mild to one person and overwhelming to another, which is why dose labelling in standard units is a key concern for regulators.

Set and setting matter as well. Anxiety, panic and a feeling of losing control are the most common acute unpleasant reactions. They are usually temporary, but they can be frightening and lead to emergency visits. A calm environment and a sober companion are often cited in harm-reduction guidance; medical help should be sought if symptoms are severe, such as chest pain, persistent vomiting, confusion or loss of consciousness.

## Risks and who should be careful

Cannabis is not harmless, and some groups carry clearly higher risk. The main risks include impaired attention and coordination, anxiety or panic, rapid heart rate, and dependence. NIDA estimates that a minority of regular users develop cannabis use disorder, and the risk is higher for those who start young and use frequently.

- **Adolescents and young adults.** The brain continues to develop into the mid-twenties. Regular use in adolescence is associated with higher risk of dependence, poorer school performance and, in some studies, cognitive effects. Avoiding use during this period is the consistent public-health advice.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Cannabinoids cross the placenta and appear in breast milk. Health agencies advise against use during pregnancy and breastfeeding because of concerns about foetal and infant development.
- **History of psychosis, schizophrenia or severe mental illness in the family.** High-potency THC and frequent use are associated with higher risk of psychotic episodes and earlier onset of illness in vulnerable people.
- **Cardiovascular disease.** THC raises heart rate and can transiently change blood pressure. People with coronary disease, arrhythmias or a history of stroke should be cautious and consult a physician.
- **Respiratory disease.** Inhaling smoke can aggravate asthma and chronic bronchitis.
- **Anyone operating machinery or caring for others.** See below.

Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome, a condition of cyclic vomiting linked to long-term heavy use, and withdrawal symptoms such as irritability, poor sleep and reduced appetite after stopping heavy use are also recognised.

## Driving and safety-critical tasks

Cannabis impairs reaction time, attention, lane control and judgement. Studies of driving performance and crash risk generally show an increase in crash risk after cannabis use, though estimates vary and are smaller than for high blood alcohol. Impairment can last several hours after inhalation and longer after edibles, and combining cannabis with alcohol increases impairment beyond either alone.

Because THC can remain detectable in blood and saliva after impairment has faded, and because no universally agreed impairment threshold exists, legal limits differ between countries. Many places apply zero tolerance or set low per-se limits. The safest approach is not to drive, cycle or operate machinery after use, and to arrange another way home.

## Interactions with medicines and other substances

THC and CBD are metabolised by liver enzymes of the cytochrome P450 family, so they can raise or lower blood levels of other drugs. Documented concerns include interactions between CBD and some anti-epileptic drugs, and between cannabis and the anticoagulant warfarin. Cannabis can also add to sedation from alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, sleeping tablets and antihistamines, and it may add to the heart-rate effect of stimulants.

Always tell a doctor or pharmacist about cannabis use before starting a new medicine or surgery. Anaesthetists in particular ask about it because it can influence anaesthetic requirements.

## Reducing harm

For people who choose to use despite the risks, public-health sources commonly suggest several principles: avoid use if in a higher-risk group, choose lower-potency products, avoid mixing with tobacco and alcohol, avoid frequent or daily use, do not drive, store products securely away from children and pets, and wait long enough with edibles before considering more. Anyone who feels unable to cut down, or who is troubled by anxiety, paranoia or mood changes, can seek help from a doctor or a local addiction service.
