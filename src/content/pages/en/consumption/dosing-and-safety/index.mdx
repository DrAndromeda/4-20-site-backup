This page is for adults and describes harm-reduction principles. It does not encourage use. Cannabis is illegal or tightly regulated in many countries, including Ukraine (see [law in Ukraine](/en/law/ukraine/)), and nothing here is medical advice. Most of the emergencies that involve cannabis come not from the drug being uniquely dangerous but from people taking more than they expected, particularly with edibles.

## Why "start low, go slow"

**The phrase means taking the smallest amount that could be enough, then waiting long enough to feel the full effect before deciding on more.** The reasoning is simple: the same dose can produce very different effects in different people, depending on body weight, genetics, tolerance, the route of intake, whether the stomach is full and the person's mood and setting. Because the effect cannot be reversed once a product is swallowed, caution at the start is the main safeguard.

Potency matters as well. Modern products can contain far more THC than the cannabis of past decades, and unregulated products may be labelled inaccurately. NIDA notes that higher THC potency raises the risk of adverse effects, and that cannabis use can lead to cannabis use disorder in some people.

These are approximate figures drawn from general pharmacology references and harm-reduction literature, and they vary between individuals and products. After swallowing, THC is processed by the liver into 11-hydroxy-THC, a metabolite that is potent and can cross into the brain readily. This is one reason edibles can feel stronger and longer-lasting than the same amount inhaled.

## Commonly cited dose ranges

**Harm-reduction guidance for people with little or no experience typically suggests a very small oral dose of THC.** A range often cited in public-health and cannabis-education materials is 2.5 to 5 mg of THC for a first oral dose, with a wait of at least two hours before even considering more. Many guides describe 10 mg as a moderate dose for experienced users, and higher doses as more likely to cause anxiety, a rapid heartbeat or impaired coordination.

These numbers are guidance, not a prescription, and they are not endorsed by any regulator as a safe dose for everybody. For orientation, Canada limits the amount of THC in an edible cannabis product to 10 mg per package, which illustrates how conservative regulated markets can be about single units. Rules in other jurisdictions differ.

Inhaled doses are harder to measure because the amount reaching the bloodstream depends on how deeply and how long a person inhales. The usual advice is the same: one small inhalation, then wait several minutes.

## Why edibles cause so many problems

**The delayed onset of edibles leads people to think nothing is happening and to take more.** Absorption through the gut is slow and variable, and a heavy meal can change timing. The result is a stacked dose that arrives all at once, with effects that last for many hours. Another risk is unlabelled or poorly labelled products: homemade edibles are rarely evenly mixed, so one piece can contain far more THC than another. Products that resemble sweets or snacks are also a risk for children.

## Signs of overconsumption and what to do

**Too much THC is usually unpleasant rather than life-threatening, but it can be frightening and sometimes needs medical help.** Possible symptoms include intense anxiety or panic, paranoia, a racing heart, dizziness, nausea and vomiting, confusion, loss of coordination and, rarely, hallucinations or psychotic symptoms.

If someone feels they have taken too much:

1. Stay calm and remind yourself that the effects are temporary.
2. Move to a quiet, safe place and sit or lie down; do not be alone.
3. Sip water; avoid taking more cannabis or any other substance, including alcohol.
4. Ask a sober person to stay and to watch for worsening symptoms.

**Call emergency services immediately** for chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting, seizure, severe confusion or agitation, persistent vomiting, or if a child or pet has swallowed cannabis. Do not wait to see if it passes. In Ukraine the general emergency number is 112.

## Alcohol, medicines and other substances

**Mixing cannabis with alcohol or sedatives increases impairment and the chance of an accident.** Alcohol and THC both slow reaction time and judgement, and combining them is associated in studies with greater impairment than either alone. Cannabis can also interact with medicines through liver enzymes (for example CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 are involved in cannabinoid metabolism), and CBD in particular is known to interfere with some drugs. People who take prescription medicines, including blood thinners and anti-seizure drugs, should speak to a physician or pharmacist. See [substances](/en/substances/) for the wider harm-reduction picture.

## Driving and safety-sensitive activities

**Do not drive, ride a bike, operate machinery or look after children while impaired.** Cannabis affects attention, reaction time and coordination, and the impairment can persist for hours after the sensation of being high fades, especially after edibles. There is no reliable rule such as "wait one hour"; the safest advice from public-health bodies is not to drive after use. Driving under the influence is an offence in most countries, and drug-driving rules often apply to any detectable amount.

## Tolerance and dependence

**Regular use leads to tolerance, meaning that more is needed for the same effect, and some people develop cannabis use disorder.** NIDA reports that a minority of users develop it, with higher risk in those who start young or use frequently. Withdrawal symptoms such as irritability, poor sleep and low appetite can occur after stopping heavy regular use. Signs that use is becoming a problem include failing to cut down, craving, and neglecting responsibilities; support is available from physicians and addiction services.

## Storage and household safety

- Keep all cannabis products in child-resistant, opaque containers, locked and out of sight.
- Store edibles separately from ordinary food and clearly labelled.
- Pets, especially dogs, can become seriously ill after eating cannabis products; contact a veterinarian promptly.
- Never share products when you do not know their strength.

## Risks and who should be careful

Avoid cannabis if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, under 25 (the brain is still developing), have a personal or family history of psychosis or schizophrenia, have serious heart or lung disease, or are taking medicines that could interact. People who are unsure should talk to a doctor.
