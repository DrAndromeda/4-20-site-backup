Readers who look into cannabis often ask about other substances in the same breath: how it compares with alcohol, whether nicotine matters, what to make of claims about medicines misused for their sedative or hallucinogenic effects. This page gives a harm-reduction overview. It does not give instructions for using anything non-medically, and it does not encourage use.

## Why comparisons need care

**Saying that one substance is "less harmful" than another can be misleading.** Harm depends on the dose, the route, the frequency, the person's age and health, the setting and the product. A substance can be less likely to cause death from a single use and still cause lasting harm through dependence, mental health effects or accidents. Comparisons help show relative risk, not safety.

## Alcohol

**Alcohol is a central nervous system depressant with well-documented toxicity.** It affects judgement and coordination, can be fatal in overdose through respiratory depression, and carries long-term risks to the liver, heart and brain along with a risk of dependence. Alcohol and cannabis are commonly combined, and the combination increases impairment, nausea and dizziness. Because both slow reaction time, driving after using either, or both, is dangerous. Public-health bodies advise against combining them.

## Nicotine

**Nicotine is highly addictive, and combining it with cannabis is common through mixed smoking.** Nicotine raises heart rate and blood pressure, and the main harm of tobacco is the smoke. People who smoke cannabis mixed with tobacco are exposed to nicotine and can develop dependence on it as well. Vaping devices deliver nicotine without combustion, but they are not harmless, and long-term effects are not fully known. 
## Diphenhydramine and similar medicines

**Diphenhydramine (often shortened to DPH) is an over-the-counter antihistamine, and recreational use of it is dangerous.** It is used for allergies, motion sickness and short-term sleep problems. It blocks histamine and also muscarinic receptors, which are involved in the nervous system and the heart. At doses well above therapeutic levels, the result can be agitation, delirium, vivid and frightening hallucinations, high body temperature, urinary retention, seizures and disturbances of heart rhythm, including prolongation of the QRS and QT intervals, which can be life-threatening. People who took too much, or are confused, have a racing or irregular heartbeat or have seizures need emergency medical help immediately.

The hallucinations that people sometimes describe are not a pleasant or controlled experience: they are a feature of anticholinergic delirium, in which the person often cannot tell what is real. This page deliberately gives no doses, because there is no safe recreational dose to describe. Do not combine diphenhydramine with alcohol, sedatives or other substances.

## Why mixing is dangerous

**Combining substances can multiply risks beyond what each would cause alone.** Common mechanisms are:

- Added sedation and slowed breathing when several depressants are used together.
- Greater impairment of judgement, coordination and reaction time.
- Extra strain on the heart, when stimulants or substances that affect heart rhythm are combined.
- Unpredictable interactions with prescription medicines.
- Difficulty recognising which substance is causing a symptom, which delays proper treatment.

## Risks and who should be careful

Anyone taking prescription medicines, who is pregnant, who has heart disease or who has a history of mental illness should speak to a doctor before using any psychoactive substance. If someone is unresponsive, has trouble breathing, seizures or chest pain after taking any substance, call emergency services at once (112 in Ukraine) and tell them what was taken. For cannabis-specific precautions, read [dosing and safety](/en/consumption/dosing-and-safety/), and see the [medical overview](/en/medical/) and the [FAQ](/en/faq/). People who want to cut down or stop can talk to a doctor or an addiction service.
