Cannabis is one of the oldest cultivated plants and one of the most chemically complex. It is grown for fibre, seed, oil and resin, and it is also among the most widely used psychoactive substances in the world. This page is an educational overview of what the plant is, how it is classified, what it contains and how humans have used it. It does not encourage use, and laws on cannabis differ widely between countries and change over time.

## What cannabis is

Cannabis is a genus of annual, usually dioecious flowering plants in the family Cannabaceae, the same family as hops (Humulus). The plant is most often classified as one species, *Cannabis sativa* L., named by Carl Linnaeus in 1753. Some taxonomists accept additional species, which is discussed in the subspecies section below.

Cannabis is wind-pollinated and typically has separate male and female plants. Female plants produce the flowers that carry the highest density of resin glands, and growers of drug-type and cannabinoid-rich plants remove males or use feminised seed to prevent pollination, because seeded flowers are less resinous. Some plants are monoecious or hermaphroditic, especially under stress or in certain fibre cultivars.

The plant is photoperiod sensitive. Most varieties flower when nights lengthen, while autoflowering varieties, which carry ruderalis genetics from high-latitude regions, flower on a fixed age schedule. This matters for both agriculture and breeding, but does not change what the plant is chemically.

## Hemp versus drug-type cannabis

Hemp and drug-type cannabis are the same species, separated mainly by THC content and by the purpose of cultivation. The distinction is defined by law and by chemistry more than by appearance.

Industrial hemp is grown for stalk fibre, seed and oil, and increasingly for cannabidiol (CBD) extraction. Fibre cultivars are planted densely, grow tall and have low resin yield. Drug-type plants are grown widely spaced, stay shorter and branch heavily to maximise flower production.

The legal line is drawn using the concentration of delta-9-THC. The European Union and many other regions have used 0.2 percent as the historical limit for hemp, with some countries and later regulations allowing 0.3 percent. The United States defines hemp as cannabis with no more than 0.3 percent delta-9-THC on a dry-weight basis. Switzerland and a few other countries use higher limits for certain products. These numbers change, so always consult the current official source in your country.

Chemically, the two groups are often described by their ratio of THC to CBD. Fibre and hemp plants usually contain little THC and, in many cases, CBD as the dominant cannabinoid. Many drug-type plants produce THC as the dominant cannabinoid, with CBD present only in traces. Intermediate types with similar amounts of both exist.

## Anatomy: which parts of the plant matter

Different parts of the plant contain very different compounds, so the part used determines the product. The plant is not uniform.

- **Flowers (inflorescences).** Unfertilised female flowers carry most of the glandular trichomes, the small resin-producing structures. These are the main source of cannabinoids and terpenes.
- **Trichomes.** Capitate-stalked trichomes are the key structures. They are microscopic, mushroom-shaped glands that synthesise and store cannabinoids and terpenes. Their appearance under magnification is used to judge maturity.
- **Leaves.** Fan leaves contain far lower concentrations than flowers. Small leaves near the flowers (sugar leaves) carry some resin.
- **Stalk.** The bark of the stem contains long bast fibres used in textiles, rope, paper and construction materials such as hempcrete. The woody core, called hurd, is used for animal bedding and insulation.
- **Seeds.** Hemp seeds are nutritious and contain around a third fat by weight, notably omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids, plus protein. Seeds contain essentially no cannabinoids, apart from traces from resin contamination.
- **Roots.** Historically used in folk medicine in some regions. They are not a significant source of cannabinoids.

## Chemistry overview

Cannabis produces a large and unusual set of secondary metabolites. The three groups most relevant to a general reader are cannabinoids, terpenes and flavonoids.

**Cannabinoids** are meroterpenoids, built from a terpene unit and a phenolic (resorcinol) unit. More than 100 have been identified. The plant makes them first as carboxylic acids such as THCA, CBDA and CBGA, which are converted to the neutral forms by heat or slowly by light and ageing. Our page on [cannabinoids](/en/cannabinoids/) covers the endocannabinoid system, the major compounds and the evidence for medical uses, and individual guides cover [THC](/en/cannabinoids/thc/) and [CBD](/en/cannabinoids/cbd/).

**Terpenes** are volatile hydrocarbons that give the plant its aroma. Reviews describe more than 200 volatile compounds, with monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes dominating. They are covered on the [terpenes](/en/terpenes/) page, including what is known and not known about their effects.

**Flavonoids** such as cannflavin A and B are specific to cannabis and have been studied for anti-inflammatory activity in laboratory work. Other classes include stilbenoids, phenolic amides and fatty acids. Most of these have little human evidence behind them.

A basic point is that chemistry in cannabis is not one number. Two flowers with identical THC percentages can have very different terpene content, minor cannabinoids and degradation state, which is one reason the same label does not guarantee a similar experience.

## A short history of cannabis

Cannabis has been used by people for thousands of years, mostly as a fibre and seed crop first and as a medicine and intoxicant later. The picture below is simplified, and some early dates rest on archaeological interpretation.

**Prehistory and antiquity.** Cord-marked pottery and hemp fibre remains from East Asia suggest early use of the plant for fibre, with some finds dated to several thousand years before the common era. Hemp textiles and rope became important in China and across Eurasia. Burial sites in the Pamir and Altai regions have yielded evidence of cannabis being burned for its smoke in ritual settings, including finds from Jirzankal in western China dated to roughly 2,500 years ago and the well-known Scythian accounts by Herodotus.

**Medicine and trade.** Cannabis appears in ancient pharmacopoeias in China and India, and in Islamic medical texts of the medieval period. Hemp was a strategic crop in Europe for ship rigging and sails, and it spread to the Americas with colonisation.

**19th century.** European physicians, including William O'Shaughnessy in India, introduced cannabis tinctures to Western medicine in the 1830s and 1840s. Cannabis preparations were sold in pharmacies in Europe and North America for a variety of conditions, though with inconsistent potency.

**20th century.** Restrictions began in the early 1900s through national laws and the International Opium Convention of 1925, then expanded after the 1961 UN Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs, which listed cannabis among controlled substances. THC was isolated and its structure determined by Raphael Mechoulam and colleagues in 1964. The discovery of the CB1 receptor in the late 1980s and early 1990s, and of anandamide in 1992, led to recognition of the endocannabinoid system.

**21st century.** Many jurisdictions have introduced medical programmes, decriminalisation or regulated adult-use markets, while others maintain strict prohibition. The WHO, EUDA and national agencies publish country-level summaries, which change regularly.

## Indica, sativa and ruderalis: what the labels really mean

The traditional three-way split is a poor guide to chemistry or effects. Botanists have long argued over whether cannabis is one species or several. Lamarck described Cannabis indica in 1785 based on plants from India, and Janischewsky described Cannabis ruderalis in 1924 from plants in Russia. Later authors, including Small and Cronquist in 1976, proposed that all cannabis is one species with subspecies and varieties.

In the commercial market, the labels evolved into something different. "Sativa" came to mean tall, narrow-leaf plants and, in marketing, energising effects. "Indica" meant shorter, broad-leaf plants and relaxing effects. "Ruderalis" refers to small, hardy, autoflowering plants from Central Europe and Russia.

Modern genetic work shows that these labels do not reliably track ancestry. Studies comparing commercial strains have found that retail names for the same label vary a lot, and that heavy crossbreeding has blurred any original lines. Chemical analyses show that a plant labelled sativa can have chemistry similar to one labelled indica. Researchers increasingly argue that classification by chemotype, meaning the dominant cannabinoid and terpene profile, is more informative than the old names. Two commonly cited chemotype groups are THC-dominant, CBD-dominant and balanced.

What this means in practice: effects depend on dose, route, individual biology, tolerance and setting, not on whether a product is called indica or sativa. Evidence that terpene content can reliably predict subjective effect is limited, as the [terpenes](/en/terpenes/) page explains.

## Risks and who should be careful

Cannabis is a psychoactive plant, and its use carries real risks that depend on dose, frequency and the person. This overview does not replace medical advice.

According to NIDA, cannabis use can impair attention, memory and coordination during intoxication, and regular use is associated with cannabis use disorder in a minority of users. The risk is higher with frequent use, high-THC products and use that starts in adolescence. NCCIH notes that evidence for medical uses is strong for only a small number of conditions and cannabinoid-based drugs, and weak or absent for many others.

People who should be especially careful include:

- adolescents and young adults, whose brains are still developing;
- people with a personal or family history of psychosis;
- pregnant or breastfeeding women;
- anyone with heart disease or taking medicines that interact with cannabinoids;
- anyone planning to drive or operate machinery.

Edible products act slowly and are a common source of overconsumption. Products from unregulated sources may be mislabelled or contaminated with pesticides, heavy metals or solvents. If you or someone you know is struggling with use, a doctor or a national helpline can help.
