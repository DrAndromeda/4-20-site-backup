Cannabidiol (CBD) is the second most studied cannabinoid after THC and the one most often found in consumer products, from oils to cosmetics. It was first isolated in the 1940s, and its structure was worked out by Raphael Mechoulam and Yuval Shvo in 1963. Unlike THC it does not cause intoxication, but that should not be confused with being risk-free or proven for the many conditions it is marketed for. This page sets out what CBD is, how it acts and where the evidence stands.

## Chemistry at a glance

CBD has the same molecular formula as THC, C₂₁H₃₀O₂, and a molar mass of about 314.5 g/mol. The difference lies in structure: in THC the molecule closes into a ring, while CBD keeps an open ring with two hydroxyl groups, which changes how it fits receptors. Like THC, it is lipophilic and poorly soluble in water, which is why oral absorption is low and variable.

In the plant, CBD is made as cannabidiolic acid (CBDA), which converts to CBD by decarboxylation when heated. Hemp cultivars are often rich in CBDA and contain little THC, which is why hemp is the main source for extraction.

## How CBD works

CBD does not act mainly through a single receptor. It has low affinity for the orthosteric site of CB1 and CB2, and some research suggests it can act as a negative allosteric modulator of CB1, which may explain why it can dampen some effects of THC in lab studies. It is also reported to interact with serotonin 5-HT1A receptors, TRPV1 channels, GPR55, adenosine signalling and PPAR-gamma, and to inhibit the breakdown of anandamide by FAAH. How much each of these contributes in people is not settled. Because it acts on so many targets, simple explanations such as "CBD boosts the endocannabinoid system" are oversimplified. See the [cannabinoids](/en/cannabinoids/) page for the system itself.

## Approved use: Epidiolex

The strongest evidence for CBD is in specific severe childhood-onset epilepsies. Epidiolex, a purified plant-derived CBD oral solution, was approved by the U.S. FDA in 2018 and later by the European Medicines Agency. It is indicated for seizures associated with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome and Dravet syndrome, and in the United States and EU also for tuberous sclerosis complex, in specified age groups, usually as an add-on to other anti-seizure medicines. In randomised controlled trials, patients taking CBD had fewer seizures than those on placebo. Common side effects include sleepiness, decreased appetite, diarrhoea and raised liver enzymes. This evidence applies to a prescription-grade, standardised product given under medical supervision, not to shop-bought oils.

## What about anxiety, pain and sleep?

Evidence for other conditions is limited and mixed. Anxiety: some small studies, including experimental studies of public-speaking stress, suggest a possible benefit, but there are few large, well-designed trials in people diagnosed with anxiety disorders. Pain: results from trials of CBD alone are inconsistent, and some of the stronger signals come from products that also contain THC. Sleep: studies are small, and sleepiness as a side effect is not the same as proven improvement in insomnia. Other claimed uses, including for acne, inflammation, addiction and neuroprotection, are largely at the preclinical or early clinical stage. NCCIH states that, apart from the approved epilepsy drug, there is not enough high-quality evidence to recommend CBD for specific health conditions. Doses used in trials also vary widely, often much higher than in typical consumer products.

## Drug interactions and liver effects

CBD can interact with other medicines, and this is one of its most practical safety issues. It is metabolised by CYP3A4 and CYP2C19 and inhibits several CYP450 enzymes, including CYP2C19 and CYP2C9, which can raise blood levels of other drugs. Known examples include clobazam (whose active metabolite increases), warfarin (raised INR has been reported), and some antidepressants and other drugs processed by these enzymes. CBD may also add to the sedating effect of alcohol, benzodiazepines and opioids. Grapefruit juice also affects some of the same enzymes.

Dose-related elevations in liver enzymes (ALT and AST) were seen in Epidiolex trials, especially in patients also taking valproate, and the label advises liver function monitoring. Lower doses in consumer products are less studied. Anyone with liver disease or taking prescription medicines should speak to a doctor before using CBD.

## Product quality and labelling

Over-the-counter CBD products are unevenly regulated, and what is in the bottle may not match the label. The FDA has tested products and found some with much less CBD than claimed, and others with detectable THC. Contaminants such as pesticides, heavy metals and residual solvents have also been reported in poorly controlled products. Terms such as "full spectrum" (contains other cannabinoids and terpenes, including some THC), "broad spectrum" (other compounds but intended to have no THC) and "isolate" (CBD only) are not tightly standardised. Full-spectrum products can contain enough THC to cause problems on drug tests or, at high intake, to cause effects. Look for independent, batch-specific certificates of analysis, and be wary of any product promising to treat or cure disease, since in most jurisdictions such claims are not allowed.

Legal status of CBD varies by country and changes. In some places only products from approved hemp sources and under a THC limit are allowed, while in others CBD is treated as a medicine. Check your national regulator for current rules.

## Risks and who should be careful

CBD is generally described as well tolerated, but adverse effects occur and the long-term data in the general population are sparse.

- **Common effects:** drowsiness, diarrhoea, changes in appetite and tiredness.
- **Liver:** raised enzymes at higher doses, particularly with valproate.
- **Interactions:** see above, which is the main practical concern for people on regular medication.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** health authorities, including the FDA, advise against use because safety data are lacking, and animal studies have shown reproductive toxicity at high doses.
- **Children:** apart from medically supervised use in epilepsy, safety in children is poorly studied.
- **Driving and work:** sedation can impair alertness, particularly in combination with other sedatives.

CBD should not replace prescribed treatment. For comparison with its intoxicating cousin, see [THC](/en/cannabinoids/thc/), and for the broader plant context, see [cannabis explained](/en/about/).
