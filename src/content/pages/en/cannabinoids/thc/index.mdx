Delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol, usually shortened to THC, is the compound mainly responsible for the intoxicating effects of cannabis. It was isolated and its structure determined in 1964 by Raphael Mechoulam and colleagues. Drug-type cannabis flower commonly contains well above 10 percent THC by dry weight, while hemp contains legally low amounts, typically a few tenths of a percent. This page covers how THC is built, how it acts, how its effects change with dose and route, and what the risks are.

## Chemistry at a glance

THC is a terpenophenolic compound with the formula C₂₁H₃₀O₂ and a molar mass of about 314.5 g/mol. It is lipophilic, almost insoluble in water and highly soluble in fats and alcohols, which affects how it is absorbed and stored in the body. In the living plant it is synthesised as THCA, which converts to THC through decarboxylation when heated.

THC and CBD share the same formula but differ in how their atoms are arranged, which explains why they act so differently. The pharmaceutical form of synthetic THC is called dronabinol.

## THCA and activation by heat

THCA is the acidic precursor and is not significantly intoxicating, because the extra carboxyl group stops it fitting well into the CB1 binding site. Heating drives off carbon dioxide and produces THC. Smoking and vaporising do this in seconds. Baking or infusing needs controlled warming, commonly cited at around 100 to 140 degrees Celsius for tens of minutes. Raw flower, juices and unheated preparations therefore contain mostly THCA. Over time, oxidation and light also turn some THC into CBN, a milder compound. More on the acids and their conversion is on the [cannabinoids](/en/cannabinoids/) page.

## How THC acts in the body

THC is a partial agonist at CB1 receptors, which are densely expressed in brain regions involved in memory, coordination and reward. It also acts on CB2 receptors and other targets. Because THC lingers at CB1 much longer than the body's own anandamide, it disturbs normal signalling in these regions. This is why acute effects can include altered perception and impaired short-term memory and motor control. Activation of CB1 in the hippocampus relates to memory effects, in the cerebellum and basal ganglia to coordination and in the amygdala to anxiety and emotion.

## Effects and their dependence on dose

THC effects are strongly dose-dependent, and the same person can have different experiences at low and high doses. Commonly reported effects at lower doses include relaxation, altered sense of time, heightened sensory perception, increased appetite, dry mouth and red eyes. At higher doses the risk rises of anxiety, panic, paranoia, dizziness, nausea, fast heart rate and impaired coordination. Very high doses, especially from edibles or concentrates, can produce acute psychotic symptoms and emergency visits, with children particularly vulnerable to accidental ingestion.

Effects also depend on tolerance, body mass, food, set and setting, concurrent alcohol or other drugs and genetic factors. Some people get unpleasant effects even at small doses. There is no universal "safe dose".

After oral intake, THC passes through the liver, where it is converted to 11-hydroxy-THC, a metabolite that reaches the brain readily and is considered more potent. This partly explains why edibles can feel stronger and last longer than the same THC dose inhaled. Delayed onset leads many people to take a second dose too soon, a common cause of overconsumption.

## Tolerance, dependence and withdrawal

Regular THC use leads to tolerance, partly because CB1 receptors become less responsive and fewer in number. Imaging studies have shown reduced CB1 availability in heavy users that tends to recover after about a month of abstinence. Some people develop cannabis use disorder, which NIDA describes as a problem pattern of use that causes distress or impairment. A commonly cited estimate is that around 3 in 10 people who use cannabis may have cannabis use disorder, and the risk is higher when use starts in adolescence and in people who use frequently. Withdrawal symptoms can include irritability, sleep problems, reduced appetite and restlessness, starting within days of stopping.

## Risks and who should be careful

THC use has well-documented risks, and several groups should be especially cautious.

- **Anxiety and panic.** Acute anxiety is among the most common adverse reactions, particularly at higher doses.
- **Cognitive effects.** Attention, learning and working memory are impaired during intoxication, and heavy long-term use is linked to small cognitive deficits in some studies, though findings on persistence after abstinence vary.
- **Adolescent brain.** The brain continues to develop into the mid-twenties. According to NIDA, regular use during adolescence is associated with poorer cognitive and educational outcomes and a greater likelihood of developing a use disorder.
- **Psychosis risk.** Observational studies link frequent use of high-potency THC to a higher risk of psychotic disorders, especially in people with a personal or family history of psychosis. Causation is debated, but those at risk are advised to avoid it.
- **Driving.** THC impairs reaction time, attention and lane control. Effects on driving can persist for several hours, and combining with alcohol raises the risk. Legal limits and impairment tests differ by country, and blood THC levels correlate poorly with impairment.
- **Heart and lungs.** THC increases heart rate and can raise cardiovascular strain, a particular concern for people with heart disease. Smoking any material irritates the airways.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** THC crosses the placenta and passes into breast milk, and health authorities recommend avoiding use.
- **Medicines.** THC is metabolised by CYP450 enzymes and may interact with sedatives, alcohol and some other drugs.

## Delta-8 and other THC variants

Delta-8-THC is a double-bond isomer of delta-9-THC, found naturally in only tiny amounts in the plant. Commercial delta-8 is usually made by chemically converting CBD, which can leave residual solvents or by-products. It is psychoactive, generally described as somewhat less potent than delta-9, and it is not an approved drug. The FDA has warned about adverse events and about products that are mislabelled. Other variants, such as THCV and THC-O acetate, are less studied, and THC-O acetate is a synthetic compound whose safety profile is largely unknown.

## Medical uses of THC

Synthetic THC (dronabinol) and the analogue nabilone are approved in some countries for chemotherapy-induced nausea and for appetite loss in certain conditions, and the THC and CBD spray nabiximols is approved for multiple sclerosis spasticity in some countries. For other conditions, such as chronic pain, evidence is mixed and benefits are modest at best, according to NCCIH. THC is not a proven treatment for anxiety, depression or sleep disorders and can worsen anxiety. Anyone with a medical condition should discuss options with a clinician.
