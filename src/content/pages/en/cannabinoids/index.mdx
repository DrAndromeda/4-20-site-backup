Cannabinoids are a family of compounds that interact with cannabinoid receptors in the body. They come from three sources: the cannabis plant (phytocannabinoids), the human body itself (endocannabinoids) and the laboratory (synthetic cannabinoids). Understanding the endocannabinoid system explains why plant compounds such as THC have such wide-ranging effects, and also why the claims around them need careful reading.

## The endocannabinoid system

The endocannabinoid system (ECS) is a signalling network that modulates the release of other neurotransmitters. It was discovered in the course of research on THC: the CB1 receptor was characterised in the late 1980s and early 1990s, CB2 followed in 1993, and the first endocannabinoid, anandamide, was identified in 1992.

The system has three parts.

- **Receptors.** CB1 receptors are among the most abundant G-protein-coupled receptors in the brain, concentrated in the hippocampus, cerebellum, basal ganglia and cortex. They sit mainly on nerve terminals and reduce neurotransmitter release. CB2 receptors are found chiefly on immune cells and in peripheral tissues, with lower levels in the brain.
- **Endocannabinoids.** The two best-known are anandamide (N-arachidonoylethanolamine) and 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG). They are made on demand from membrane lipids, act locally, and are cleared quickly. 2-AG is present at much higher concentrations in the brain than anandamide.
- **Enzymes.** Fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) breaks down anandamide, and monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL) breaks down most 2-AG. Drugs that block these enzymes have been tested in trials, and so far they have not delivered clear clinical benefits.

The ECS is involved in regulating pain perception, appetite, memory, stress responses, sleep and inflammation. This is a broad physiological role, which is a reason to expect many effects from cannabinoids but not a reason to assume any of them are therapeutic. Many compounds also act on other targets, including TRP channels, PPAR receptors, serotonin receptors and GPR55.

## Phytocannabinoids

More than 100 phytocannabinoids have been identified in cannabis, though only a handful occur in significant amounts. They are synthesised in glandular trichomes from olivetolic acid and a terpene precursor, geranyl pyrophosphate. The first product is cannabigerolic acid (CBGA), the common precursor from which specific enzymes produce THCA, CBDA and CBCA.

Most phytocannabinoids have a 21-carbon skeleton and are lipophilic, meaning they dissolve in fats and oils rather than water. That is why oral bioavailability is low and variable, and why taking cannabinoids with a fatty meal can substantially raise absorption.

## Acidic precursors and decarboxylation

In the living plant most cannabinoids exist as carboxylic acids, and heat converts them into the neutral forms that are active at CB1. THCA, for example, has a carboxyl group that prevents it from fitting the CB1 binding site effectively.

Decarboxylation is the loss of that carboxyl group as carbon dioxide. It happens slowly at room temperature, and faster as temperature rises. Smoking and vaporising decarboxylate almost instantly. For cooking, published lab work reports that conversion needs moderate temperatures over tens of minutes, with ranges of about 100 to 140 degrees Celsius commonly cited. Higher temperatures speed conversion but also increase loss of terpenes and degradation of THC into CBN.

Acids are not inert. THCA and CBDA are being studied separately, and some preclinical work suggests activity at targets other than CB1. Evidence in people is minimal, and most acid-based products are sold without clinical data.

CBN is produced when THC degrades through oxidation, so it is more prevalent in aged material. Despite marketing around sleep, controlled human evidence for CBN is very limited. CBG is called the "mother cannabinoid" because CBGA is the precursor to the others, and most plants carry it at low levels, although specific cultivars have been bred to accumulate it.

## Entourage effect: an idea with limited evidence

The entourage effect is the hypothesis that the full set of compounds in cannabis acts together to produce effects that isolated compounds do not. The term was introduced in 1998 by Ben-Shabat and Mechoulam in the context of endocannabinoids and was later applied by others to whole-plant cannabis, including cannabinoid-terpene interactions.

There is some support. In animal and cell experiments, CBD can modify some effects of THC, and terpene and cannabinoid mixtures can behave differently from single compounds. Some patients and clinicians report differences between whole-plant extracts and isolates. The clinical picture is less clear:

- Studies in people are few, small and often not blinded or controlled properly.
- Many reported terpene effects occur at concentrations far above those found in typical products.
- Whole-plant extracts vary by batch, which makes trials hard to replicate.
- Some comparisons show that the extra compounds add side effects without extra benefit.

A fair summary is that the entourage effect is a plausible hypothesis that remains under investigation. Claims that a "full spectrum" product is better than an isolate are, at this point, not supported by solid clinical evidence. See [terpenes](/en/terpenes/) for the related discussion.

## Approved cannabinoid medicines

A small number of cannabinoid-based drugs have regulatory approval for specific conditions. This is the part of the field where evidence is strongest, and it is much narrower than the range of conditions for which cannabis products are marketed.

- **Epidiolex (cannabidiol oral solution).** Approved in the United States by the FDA in 2018, and later in the European Union, for seizures associated with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, Dravet syndrome and tuberous sclerosis complex, in the age groups specified on the label. Randomised controlled trials showed reductions in seizure frequency compared with placebo. Liver enzyme elevations and drug interactions with clobazam are recognised issues.
- **Nabiximols (Sativex).** An oromucosal spray with roughly equal amounts of THC and CBD from cannabis extracts. It is approved in a number of countries, including several in Europe, as an add-on for moderate to severe spasticity in multiple sclerosis when other treatments have not worked. It is not approved in the United States.
- **Dronabinol (Marinol and others).** Synthetic delta-9-THC capsules approved in the United States for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting and for anorexia associated with weight loss in AIDS.
- **Nabilone (Cesamet).** A synthetic THC analogue approved in several countries, including the United States and Canada, for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting when other treatments fail.

NCCIH notes that outside these approved uses, the evidence for cannabis or cannabinoids as treatment is limited or inconclusive for most conditions. Evidence for chronic pain is mixed, with modest benefits in some reviews and significant side effects. Smoked or vaped cannabis and over-the-counter CBD products are not approved drugs, and the FDA has warned that such products vary in content and quality.

## Risks, safety and who should be careful

Cannabinoids are pharmacologically active, and side effects and interactions are real. Common adverse effects of THC-containing products include dizziness, dry mouth, fast heart rate, anxiety and impaired memory and coordination. CBD products can cause drowsiness, diarrhoea and decreased appetite, and may raise liver enzymes at higher doses.

Important cautions:

- **Drug interactions.** CBD and THC are metabolised by liver enzymes of the CYP450 family and can alter blood levels of other medicines, such as some anticonvulsants, anticoagulants like warfarin and certain antidepressants. Ask a pharmacist or doctor.
- **Mental health.** High-dose or frequent THC use is associated with anxiety and, in vulnerable people, a higher risk of psychosis. See the [THC page](/en/cannabinoids/thc/) for details.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Cannabinoids cross the placenta and are found in breast milk. Health authorities advise against use in these periods.
- **Young people.** Heavy use in adolescence is linked to worse outcomes in school performance and a higher chance of cannabis use disorder.
- **Driving.** THC impairs reaction time and coordination, and its effects can last for hours after use. Impaired driving is a legal and safety issue in most countries.
- **Product quality.** Unregulated products may contain different amounts of cannabinoids than the label states, and may contain contaminants. The FDA has reported that many CBD products it tested did not match their labels.

Nothing on this page is medical advice. Anyone considering a cannabinoid medicine for a health condition should talk to a qualified clinician, especially if they take other medicines. For general background on the plant, see [cannabis explained](/en/about/).
