faq:
  - q: "Is cannabis addictive?"
    a: "Some people develop cannabis use disorder, which involves craving, difficulty cutting down and withdrawal symptoms such as irritability and poor sleep. NIDA reports that risk is higher in people who start young or use frequently. Most users do not become dependent, but the risk is real."
  - q: "How long does THC stay detectable?"
    a: "It depends on the test, frequency of use and the individual. THC is detectable in blood for a relatively short time, while metabolites can remain in urine for days or, after heavy regular use, several weeks. Hair tests look back much further. No single figure applies to everyone."
  - q: "What is the difference between hemp and marijuana?"
    a: "Both are Cannabis sativa. Hemp is a legal and regulatory term for varieties with very low THC content, grown for fibre, seed or CBD; the exact threshold varies by country. Marijuana refers to cannabis bred for higher THC. Botanically they are the same species."
  - q: "How is CBD different from THC?"
    a: "THC is the main intoxicating cannabinoid and acts on CB1 receptors. CBD does not produce a high and has a different pharmacology. One CBD medicine, Epidiolex, is approved for certain seizure disorders. CBD can still interact with other medicines and is not risk-free."
  - q: "Is vaping cannabis safer than smoking it?"
    a: "Vaping avoids combustion by-products such as tar, so it may expose lungs to fewer of them. That does not make it safe. Contaminated or illicit vape products have been linked to serious lung injury, and long-term effects of vaping cannabis are not well established."
  - q: "Does cannabis affect memory?"
    a: "Acute intoxication impairs short-term memory and attention, because THC acts on CB1 receptors in memory-related brain regions. Studies suggest heavy use, especially beginning in adolescence, is associated with poorer cognitive performance, though recovery after stopping and the extent of lasting effects remain debated."
  - q: "Can you overdose on cannabis?"
    a: "A fatal overdose from THC alone is considered very rare, but taking too much can cause panic, vomiting, a racing heart, confusion and sometimes psychotic symptoms that need medical care. Edibles, high-potency products and children's accidental ingestion are the main concerns."
  - q: "Is cannabis legal?"
    a: "It depends on the country. Some jurisdictions permit regulated adult use or medical use, while many prohibit it. In Ukraine it is a controlled substance, and a medical reform was adopted in late 2024. Laws change, so check official sources for the current position."
  - q: "Can I drive after using cannabis?"
    a: "No. Cannabis slows reaction time and impairs attention and coordination, and impairment can outlast the feeling of being high, especially after edibles. Public-health bodies advise against driving after use, and drug-driving is an offence in most countries."
  - q: "Is cannabis safe during pregnancy?"
    a: "Health authorities advise against it. THC crosses the placenta, and studies have linked prenatal exposure to lower birth weight and to possible developmental effects. Anyone who is pregnant, trying to conceive or breastfeeding should speak with a doctor about their situation."
  - q: "What is the difference between medical and recreational cannabis?"
    a: "Medical cannabis means use under medical supervision for a defined condition, often with standardised products or approved drugs. Recreational use is non-medical. The plant is the same, but medical use involves dosing, monitoring and a regulated supply chain, and evidence supports only certain conditions."

People come to a cannabis reference with very practical questions, and many of them have simple, honest answers that are buried under hype or fear. The questions on this page are the ones that come up most often: whether cannabis is addictive, how long it can be detected, how hemp differs from marijuana, what separates CBD from THC, whether vaping is safer, what happens to memory, whether an overdose is possible, what the law says, why driving is a problem, what pregnancy means for the question, and how medical use differs from recreational use.

Each answer below is deliberately brief. It tells you what is reasonably well established, where the evidence is thin and where the answer depends on the person, the product or the country. Short answers cannot cover every circumstance, so each one is paired with a longer page on the site.

## How to use these answers

**Treat every answer as a starting point, not a conclusion.** Cannabis research is uneven: some topics, such as the impairment caused by intoxication, are well documented, while others, such as the long-term effects of newer high-potency products, are still being studied. Where the evidence is limited, the answers say so.

For the effects of the main intoxicating compound, read [THC](/en/cannabinoids/thc/), and for its non-intoxicating counterpart, [CBD](/en/cannabinoids/cbd/). The page on [dosing and safety](/en/consumption/dosing-and-safety/) explains harm-reduction principles in detail, including edible timing, alcohol, driving and storage. The [medical overview](/en/medical/) sets out which therapeutic claims are backed by evidence and which are not, and the page on [law in Ukraine](/en/law/ukraine/) describes the legal position in general terms.

## Medical and legal disclaimers

**Nothing here replaces advice from a doctor or a lawyer.** If you have a health concern, a medical condition, take medicines or are pregnant, talk to a qualified professional. If you are in a legal situation, consult a lawyer. In an emergency, call your local emergency number; in Ukraine that is 112.

## Where the facts come from

Statements are based on public-health and scientific bodies such as NIDA, NCCIH, the EUDA and the WHO, and on the published literature.
