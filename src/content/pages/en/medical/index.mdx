Medical cannabis is a broad label that covers very different things: approved pharmaceutical drugs made from or modelled on cannabinoids, standardised plant extracts, and herbal cannabis prescribed in some countries. The evidence differs sharply between them, and between conditions. This overview separates what is established from what remains uncertain.

## Approved cannabinoid medicines

**A small number of cannabinoid-based medicines have been approved by regulators for specific conditions.** A purified cannabidiol oral solution (marketed as Epidiolex in the United States) is approved for seizures associated with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, Dravet syndrome and tuberous sclerosis complex. Dronabinol, a synthetic form of THC, and nabilone, a synthetic THC-like compound, have been approved in some countries for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting. Nabiximols, a plant-derived oral-mucosal spray containing THC and CBD, is authorised in a number of countries for spasticity in multiple sclerosis. Approval status differs between countries, so the local regulator is the authority.

The labels are broad summaries and individual trials vary in size and quality. Conditions such as cancer itself, Alzheimer's disease or depression do not have robust evidence that cannabis treats them, and claims that it cures disease are unsupported.

## Why physician supervision matters

**Cannabinoids have real pharmacology, which means real side effects and interactions.** THC can cause dizziness, drowsiness, a fast heartbeat, anxiety and impaired thinking. CBD can cause fatigue, diarrhoea and changes in liver enzymes, and it interacts with several medicines, including some anti-seizure drugs and blood thinners. People with a personal or family history of psychosis, heart disease or substance use disorder face higher risks. A physician can weigh these risks, monitor dose and outcomes and offer proven alternatives. The harm-reduction principles in [dosing and safety](/en/consumption/dosing-and-safety/) apply here too.

## Medical access and the law

Rules for medical cannabis vary greatly worldwide, from tightly regulated prescription systems to prohibition. For the position in Ukraine, including the reform adopted in late 2024, see [law in Ukraine](/en/law/ukraine/) and check official sources.

## Risks and who should be careful

Pregnant or breastfeeding people, adolescents, older adults with heart conditions and anyone taking multiple medicines should not self-medicate. Self-treatment may delay effective care for serious conditions.
