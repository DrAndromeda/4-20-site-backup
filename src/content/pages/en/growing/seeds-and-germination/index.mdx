**Legal note.** Possession of cannabis seeds and the cultivation of cannabis are illegal or restricted in many countries, and the rules for seeds differ from those for plants. This page is a botany overview, not an invitation to grow. Check current law with an official source; see the [law overview](/en/law/).

## What a cannabis seed is

A cannabis seed is technically an achene, a small dry fruit containing one seed. Inside the hard shell (pericarp) are the embryo, with two embryonic leaves (cotyledons) and a root tip (radicle), and a store of oils and proteins that feed the plant until the first leaves can photosynthesise. Seeds are typically a few millimetres long, oval, and often marked with a mottled or striped pattern.

The seed is dormant until it receives the right cues. Water softens the shell and activates enzymes that mobilise stored reserves, oxygen enables respiration, and warmth speeds up metabolism. Light is not required for germination, and darkness is common in practice.

Seed biology also explains why commercial seed is a regulated item in agriculture: certified hemp seed lots are tested for variety identity and THC compliance. See the [growing overview](/en/growing/) for where seeds fit into the plant's whole cycle.

## Seed quality and viability

A viable seed is mature, intact and healthy; most failures start before the seed reaches any medium. Visual cues commonly used to judge seed quality include:

- **Colour.** Mature seeds are usually brown, grey or dark with mottling. Pale green or white seeds are often immature.
- **Hardness.** Seeds should feel firm and resist light pressure. Soft or easily crushed seeds are often empty or poor.
- **Shell integrity.** Cracks, mould or damage reduce viability.
- **Weight.** Heavier seeds are generally better filled, although this is not a rule.

The traditional float test, where seeds that stay on the surface of water are considered poor, is unreliable, since a trapped air film can keep good seeds afloat. Germination rate is the real measure, usually expressed as the percentage of seeds that sprout under standard conditions.

Viability falls over time. Seeds that are stored warm, humid or exposed to light lose viability faster. As a general guide seeds stored cool and dry can remain usable for years, but germination percentages decline gradually, and seeds several years old often sprout more slowly and less evenly.

## Storage

Storage conditions decide how long seeds stay viable. The general principle for seeds of most crops is a cool, dark, dry and stable environment.

- **Temperature.** Cool and constant, commonly in the range of about 5 to 10 °C for medium-term storage. Fluctuations are harmful.
- **Humidity.** Low, with seeds kept dry before sealing. Moisture encourages mould and early sprouting.
- **Light.** Dark containers prevent degradation.
- **Container.** Airtight and clearly labelled, with silica gel sometimes used to keep humidity down.

Seeds should not be frozen unless they are properly dried first, since ice crystals can damage cells. Storage rules are the same as for most vegetable seeds, which is a useful comparison for beginners in botany.

## Germination methods

Two approaches are most commonly described: the paper-towel method and direct sowing into a substrate. Both rely on the same principles of warmth, moisture and oxygen.

**Paper towel method.** The seed is placed between layers of moist paper towel, often on a plate and covered to hold humidity, and kept warm. Its advantage is that the grower can watch the taproot emerge and can discard seeds that do not sprout. The drawbacks are the risk of damaging the delicate root during transfer and of the towel drying out or becoming too wet.

**Direct sowing.** The seed is placed about 0.5 to 1 cm deep in a lightly moist, loose, fine-textured medium such as a seedling mix, and the taproot grows downward without being moved. This reduces handling stress, but the grower cannot see progress and a failed seed may go unnoticed for longer.

Other methods exist, such as soaking seeds in water for up to about 12 to 24 hours to soften the shell, or using small peat or rockwool plugs. Long soaking can drown seeds because they run out of oxygen, so it is not recommended beyond a day.

## Temperature, humidity and timeline

Germination is fastest at about 22 to 25 °C and in a medium that is constantly damp but never waterlogged. Relative humidity around the seed or seedling is often kept at roughly 70 to 90 percent, for example with a humidity dome, and eased gradually afterwards.

The usual timeline is as follows. The shell cracks and the white taproot appears in 1 to 3 days for fresh seeds, often up to about 7 days for others, and occasionally 10 days or more for older ones. The seedling then pushes up and the cotyledons open within a few days of emerging from the medium. After the cotyledons, the first true leaves appear, typically with a single blade, then three or more. Seedlings are fragile and sensitive to overwatering, strong light and drafts.

## Common failures

Most failures can be traced to moisture, temperature or seed quality.

- **Too wet.** Waterlogged conditions deprive the seed of oxygen and encourage rot and damping-off fungi.
- **Too dry.** If the seed dries out after the process starts, the embryo can die.
- **Too cold or too hot.** Below about 18 °C germination slows greatly, and above about 30 °C seeds and seedlings suffer.
- **Old or immature seeds.** These simply may not have the energy reserves.
- **Handling damage.** Touching or bending the taproot, or planting upside down, delays emergence.
- **Seedling stretch.** Weak light makes seedlings tall and thin.
- **Damping-off.** Seedlings collapse at soil level because of fungal infection in wet, cool, stagnant conditions.

After the seedling establishes, the plant enters the next stage described on the [lifecycle page](/en/growing/lifecycle/).

## Legal context

Seed law is inconsistent. In some countries, seeds are sold as souvenirs or for collection where germination is banned, in others both possession and germination are criminal offences, and in others only authorised producers can use certified hemp seeds. Importing seeds across borders is regulated almost everywhere. Because these rules differ so much and change, consult your government's legal sources.
