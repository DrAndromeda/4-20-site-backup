## Stages in brief

A cannabis plant completes its life in one season and moves through six recognisable stages. Duration depends on genetics, light cycle and conditions, so the figures below are typical ranges, not fixed values.

## Germination

Germination is the emergence of the root from the seed and takes about 1 to 7 days in warm, moist conditions. The taproot extends first, and then the seed coat opens and the stem lifts the first seed leaves above the medium. Details of seed quality and methods are on the [seeds and germination page](/en/growing/seeds-and-germination/).

## Seedling stage

The seedling stage lasts about two to three weeks, during which the plant lives off its cotyledons and then builds its first true leaves. The first true leaves have a single blade with serrated edges, followed by leaves with three, then five, then more fingers as growth continues.

Seedlings are delicate. They have small root systems, so they are sensitive to overwatering and to temperature swings, and they typically do best with moderate light and relatively high humidity, around 65 to 75 percent. Air temperatures near 22 to 26 °C are typical.

## Vegetative stage

Vegetative growth is the period of fast increase in size, in which the plant builds roots, stem, branches and leaves. Photoperiod plants stay vegetative as long as they receive roughly 16 to 18 hours of light daily, which is why this stage can be short or long. Many horticulturalists use 18/6, and some prefer 24 hours of light, though a dark period is often considered beneficial for plant rest and energy use.

During this stage the plant's nitrogen needs are high, and training techniques such as topping or bending are used in cultivation to shape the plant. These manipulations are specific to the grower's goals and are outside the scope of this page. Temperatures are commonly 22 to 28 °C with humidity gradually lowered from about 70 percent to 50 percent.

## Pre-flower

Pre-flowering is the transition in which the plant shows its sex, usually after about four to six weeks of life for photoperiod plants once they are mature enough to respond to light changes. Small structures form at the nodes where leaf stalks meet the stem. Female plants show pairs of white, hair-like stigmas emerging from a small calyx, while male plants show small round pollen sacs that look like clusters of tiny balls.

Sex expression is mainly genetic. Cannabis is dioecious, with separate male and female plants, which is why seed-based crops contain both sexes unless the seeds are feminised. Only unfertilised female flowers develop into resinous clusters, and pollen can cause fertilisation and seeds.

## Flowering stage

Flowering lasts typically 8 to 11 weeks for photoperiod plants, though some varieties take longer. The light cycle is switched to 12 hours of light and 12 hours of uninterrupted dark to trigger flowering indoors, while outdoors flowering follows the autumn shortening of days. Autoflowering plants begin flowering on their own schedule, commonly after three to five weeks.

The plant grows quickly in the first two or three weeks, stretching in height, and then flowers swell and resin builds. Temperatures are commonly 20 to 26 °C early on and lower later in the stage, with humidity around 40 to 50 percent to reduce the risk of mould. Leaves may yellow naturally near the end as the plant stops growing.

Light plays a central role: even small leaks during the dark period can interrupt flowering in sensitive plants, and a plant that is returned to long days will revert to vegetative growth in many cases.

## Sex expression and hermaphroditism

Hermaphroditism is the appearance of both female and male flowers on a single plant, and it is usually a stress response. Triggers include light leaks, extreme heat, physical damage, prolonged stress, plant age and genetic predisposition. Pollen from such plants can fertilise flowers of the same plant and neighbouring ones, which causes seeds and reduces the quality of seedless flowers.

Hermaphrodites may show either as full male flowers among female ones or as nanners, small yellow banana-shaped pollen-bearing structures. Vigilant inspection is therefore part of the process in cultivation. In seed production, controlled approaches are used to make feminised seeds, which is outside the scope of this page.

## Trichome maturity

Trichomes are the small glandular hairs on flowers and sugar leaves that produce cannabinoids and terpenes. The capitate-stalked type is the most relevant, with a stalk and a round head. Under a loupe or microscope the head changes colour as it ripens: clear, then cloudy or milky, then amber.

In practice, clear trichomes are considered immature, cloudy ones indicate near-peak THC content, and amber trichomes suggest ageing and conversion towards CBN, a degradation product of THC. Many people also watch the stigmas, which darken and curl back. These are practical rules of thumb rather than exact measurements, and cannabinoid profile also depends on genetics and growing conditions. For the compounds themselves see the [THC page](/en/cannabinoids/thc/).

## Harvest and what follows

Harvest happens when most trichomes have reached the desired state and the flowers are fully developed. The plant is cut, then dried slowly, typically over one to two weeks in cool, dark, ventilated conditions, and cured. See the [growing overview](/en/growing/) for post-harvest basics, and the [consumption page](/en/consumption/) for health information related to use.
