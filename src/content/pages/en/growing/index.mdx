**Legal note.** Growing cannabis is a criminal offence in many countries and is tightly regulated in others. Rules differ between countries and even between states or regions, and they change. This page describes the plant's biology and general horticulture for educational purposes only. It is not a cultivation guide and not legal advice; check your national or local law first. See the [overview of cannabis law](/en/law/).

## Cannabis as a crop plant

Cannabis (Cannabis sativa L.) is an annual, wind-pollinated flowering plant that has been grown for fibre, seed oil and resin for thousands of years. Its horticulture resembles that of other fast-growing annual crops: it needs light, water, mineral nutrients, a suitable root zone and enough air movement to keep disease down.

Most plants are dioecious, meaning individual plants are male or female. Only unpollinated female plants produce the large resin-rich flower clusters that are grown for cannabinoid content, while male plants produce pollen sacs. Fibre and seed crops, by contrast, are usually grown as dense stands where sex does not matter.

The plant's chemistry also depends on genetics and environment. Cannabinoid and terpene profiles are largely inherited, but light intensity, temperature, nutrition, stress and harvest timing all influence the final result. For the chemistry itself, see the pages on [THC](/en/cannabinoids/thc/) and other cannabinoids.

## Photoperiod versus autoflowering plants

The key choice in cultivation biology is how the plant decides to flower: by day length or by age.

Photoperiod plants are short-day plants. Under long days (commonly 16 to 18 hours of light) they remain in vegetative growth and keep adding leaves and branches. When the uninterrupted dark period lengthens to about 12 hours, flowering is triggered. This is why growers working indoors describe an 18/6 cycle (18 hours light, 6 dark) for vegetative growth and a 12/12 cycle for flowering. Outdoors, the same effect comes from the shortening days after the summer solstice.

Autoflowering plants carry genes from Cannabis ruderalis, a northern form adapted to short growing seasons. They begin to flower after a certain number of weeks regardless of the light cycle, so they can be kept under long light for most or all of their life. They are usually smaller and finish faster, but the lack of a controllable vegetative stage limits how much they can be trained or recovered from setbacks.

## Indoor and outdoor cultivation

Indoor growing gives control over light, temperature and humidity, while outdoor growing relies on the local climate and sun. Each has its own trade-offs.

Indoors, the grower supplies all the light, usually with LED or high-intensity discharge lamps, and controls climate with fans, filters and ventilation. Plants can be cycled at any time of year, and sensitive variables such as the dark period are easy to protect. The costs are electricity, equipment and the need to manage heat, humidity and odour. Light leaks during the dark period can disturb flowering in photoperiod plants.

Outdoors, sunlight is free and plants can become large, but the grower is exposed to weather, pests, mould in wet autumns and the calendar. Latitude matters: in the northern hemisphere, a plant started too late will not have time to build size, and one finishing in a cold, damp October may suffer bud rot. Greenhouses sit in between, extending the season while still using natural light.

Growing media matter as well. Soil and soil-like mixes buffer nutrients and water and are forgiving. Soilless media such as coco coir or perlite blends, and hydroponic systems, deliver nutrients in solution, which gives faster growth but leaves less margin for error in concentration and pH.

Humidity is usually described through relative humidity, but the physiologically relevant factor is the vapour-pressure deficit, which combines temperature and humidity. High humidity late in flowering favours fungal diseases such as grey mould, so ranges are lowered as the flowers become denser. Temperatures well above about 30 °C slow growth, stress plants and can reduce quality, while sustained cold slows metabolism.

Light intensity increases from a gentle level for seedlings to higher levels for vegetative and flowering plants. Too much light with insufficient airflow or too little water leads to leaf damage, while too little light produces stretched, weak growth.

## Root-zone pH and nutrition

Nutrient uptake depends heavily on the pH of the root zone. For soil the commonly quoted range is 6.0 to 7.0. For hydroponic and soilless systems it is lower, about 5.5 to 6.5, because roots are in direct contact with solution.

Plants need macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium), secondary nutrients (calcium, magnesium, sulphur) and trace elements such as iron, zinc and manganese. In general, nitrogen demand is higher during vegetative growth, while phosphorus and potassium become relatively more important in flowering. Over-feeding is as common a problem as under-feeding and shows up as dark, clawed leaf tips or burnt tips. Because the pH of runoff can drift, it is usually measured with a meter or test drops and the electrical conductivity of the solution is tracked in soilless systems.

Water quality matters too: very hard tap water may raise pH and leave mineral deposits, and chlorine can affect root-zone life. Containers need drainage, because waterlogged roots suffer from low oxygen.

## Lifecycle at a glance

From seed to harvest the plant passes through germination, seedling, vegetative growth, pre-flowering, flowering and harvest. Germination takes a few days, the seedling stage about two to three weeks, vegetative growth several weeks to months, and flowering usually 8 to 11 weeks for photoperiod plants. A closer look is on the [lifecycle page](/en/growing/lifecycle/), and seed biology is covered in [seeds and germination](/en/growing/seeds-and-germination/).

Seedlings are generally kept indoors or protected until the risk of frost has passed, which in much of Europe and North America means May. Flowering begins as nights lengthen after the June solstice, becomes visible in late summer, and the harvest comes from late September into October depending on genetics and weather. Early-finishing types can be ready sooner, which helps in regions with wet autumns. Autoflowering types can be sown later and finish within a few months.

## Common problems

Most failures come from a short list of causes: overwatering, wrong pH, nutrient imbalance, heat and humidity extremes, pests and disease.

- **Overwatering.** Drooping, heavy leaves in a constantly wet pot are the classic sign. Roots need air as well as water.
- **Nutrient deficiency or lockout.** Yellowing lower leaves often point to nitrogen shortage, but similar symptoms appear when incorrect pH blocks uptake even though nutrients are present.
- **Nutrient burn.** Tips turn yellow or brown after too strong a feed.
- **Light or heat stress.** Bleached tops, curled leaf edges or foxtailing flowers can follow excessive light or heat.
- **Pests.** Spider mites, fungus gnats, aphids and thrips are the usual culprits. Prevention, quarantine of new plants and regular inspection are more effective than late treatment.
- **Mould and mildew.** Powdery mildew and grey mould (Botrytis) thrive in humid, stagnant air and can ruin dense flowers.
- **Hermaphroditism.** Stress such as light leaks, heat or damage can make a female plant produce male flowers and pollinate others. See the [lifecycle page](/en/growing/lifecycle/).
- **Pollination.** Seeds appear in flowers if pollen reaches them, which lowers cannabinoid content per weight in seed-free crops.

## Harvest timing and post-harvest basics

Harvest timing is judged mainly by the trichomes, the resin glands on the flowers, viewed under magnification. They change from clear to cloudy (milky) to amber as they mature, and cannabinoid composition shifts along with them. The basics are explained on the [lifecycle page](/en/growing/lifecycle/).

After cutting, plants are dried slowly in a cool, dark, ventilated space, commonly for one to two weeks, and then cured in closed containers while moisture equalises. Drying too fast leaves chlorophyll and harsh taste, while drying too slowly invites mould. Stored material should be kept cool, dark and dry, and out of reach of children and pets.

## Legal status and responsibility

Cultivation is the area where laws are strictest and most varied. In many countries, growing any plant with more than a small THC content is prohibited and carries penalties that can include prison. Other jurisdictions allow a limited number of plants for adults or registered patients, usually with rules about where plants may be kept, who may see them and how much may be possessed. Industrial hemp varieties with low THC are legal to grow in many countries but typically require a licence and approved seed varieties.

Because rules change, always consult official sources such as your government's legal portal or health ministry. The [law overview](/en/law/) explains the general models. If you live somewhere cultivation is not permitted, this information is for understanding only.
