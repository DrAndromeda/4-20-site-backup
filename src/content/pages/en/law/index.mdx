This page describes in general terms how cannabis is regulated. It is information, not legal advice, and it is not a guide to avoiding the law. Laws change frequently and differ within countries, so verify anything that affects you on an official source.

## The international framework

Almost every country is party to UN drug-control treaties that classify cannabis as a controlled substance, and these treaties set the outer limits of national policy. Three conventions matter most.

The Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs of 1961 (amended by a 1972 protocol) is the foundation. It placed cannabis and cannabis resin among controlled drugs and obliged states to limit production, trade and possession to medical and scientific purposes. The Convention on Psychotropic Substances of 1971 covers synthetic substances including THC itself in its schedules. The 1988 Convention against Illicit Traffic in Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances requires states to criminalise trafficking, and, subject to each state's constitutional principles, to treat deliberate possession and cultivation for personal consumption as an offence.

The treaties leave room for interpretation, and countries have used it differently: some decriminalise personal use, some tolerate sales, and some have introduced regulated markets that critics say conflict with the treaties. In December 2020 the UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs voted to remove cannabis from the most restrictive Schedule IV of the 1961 Convention, acknowledging its therapeutic potential, though it remains under international control.

## A spectrum of national models

Policies can be grouped into four broad models, though real systems often combine them.

- **Prohibition.** Possession, supply and cultivation are criminal offences. Penalties range from fines to long prison terms, and in a few countries can be severe.
- **Decriminalisation or depenalisation.** Personal possession is not prosecuted as a crime, or is treated as an administrative offence, while trade stays illegal.
- **Medical programmes.** Cannabis or cannabinoid medicines may be prescribed to patients, either as licensed pharmaceuticals or through a regulated medical supply scheme.
- **Regulated adult use.** The state licenses production and sale to adults, with age limits, packaging and advertising rules, and taxation.

## Hemp and THC thresholds

Industrial hemp is cannabis grown for fibre, seed and other uses, and is separated from drug cannabis mainly by THC content. Most thresholds are expressed as a percentage of delta-9 THC in the plant or flowering tops.

The European Union allows direct payments for hemp varieties registered in the common catalogue with a THC content up to 0.3 percent. In the United States, the 2018 Farm Bill removed hemp, defined as cannabis with no more than 0.3 percent delta-9 THC on a dry-weight basis, from the federal list of controlled substances. Other countries use 0.2 percent, 1 percent or higher, and some apply separate limits to food products and extracts such as CBD oil. Hemp grown for fibre typically needs a licence and certified seed in most jurisdictions.

These thresholds are a legal definition, not a biological line, and a plant can be legal hemp in one country and a controlled drug in another. Related products such as CBD extracts, hemp-derived THC analogues and edibles are regulated differently again, often as foods or novel foods, and rules are evolving.

## Country examples

The examples below are simplified and reflect the position as generally understood at the time of writing. They may have changed.

**Netherlands.** Cannabis remains illegal, but a long-standing tolerance policy (gedoogbeleid) means that prosecution is not pursued for small amounts sold in licensed coffeeshops under strict criteria such as no advertising and a limit on stock. The coffeeshops are supplied by an illegal back door, which has been widely criticised. A government experiment with regulated supply to selected coffeeshops in several municipalities started in the 2020s to study a controlled chain.

**Canada.** The Cannabis Act came into force in October 2018 and made Canada the first G7 country to legalise adult use nationally. It sets a federal framework, with provinces and territories deciding on retail models, minimum age and home cultivation rules. Medical access has existed since the early 2000s.

**Uruguay.** In 2013 Uruguay became the first country to regulate the entire cannabis market. Adults who are residents can obtain cannabis through pharmacies, registered home growing or cannabis clubs, under state oversight and registration.

**Germany.** A law that took effect in April 2024 removed cannabis from the Narcotics Act for adults, permitting limited possession and home cultivation and allowing non-profit cultivation associations, while commercial retail was not introduced. Medical cannabis rules were also changed. The political debate over further changes has been ongoing, so current rules must be checked.

**United States.** Cannabis remains a controlled substance under federal law, while a majority of states have medical programmes and many have adult-use regulation. This creates a federal and state conflict that affects banking, interstate trade and employment. Federal scheduling has been under review, so the situation can change.

**Thailand.** In 2022 Thailand removed cannabis from its narcotics list, and a wave of home growing and retail followed. Authorities then tightened rules, notably restricting recreational sale and requiring prescriptions or licences, and policy has continued to shift.

**Spain.** Private consumption in private places is not a criminal offence, while sale and public consumption are punished by fines or crimes. Cannabis social clubs operate in a legal grey area, and court rulings have narrowed their room for manoeuvre.

**Ukraine.** Possession of small amounts of cannabis is subject to administrative liability, while larger amounts, cultivation and trafficking are criminal. Reforms for medical use have been discussed and adopted in stages, so the details are best checked on official sources.

**Russia.** Cannabis is strictly controlled. Small amounts are generally dealt with administratively, while larger amounts, cultivation and supply are criminal offences with substantial penalties.

## Medical cannabis regimes

Medical access is the most widely accepted exception to prohibition. Approaches fall into two groups. In the first, specific cannabinoid medicines are approved by drug regulators after clinical trials. Examples include the plant-derived CBD medicine approved for certain severe childhood epilepsies, and synthetic or plant-derived THC-based medicines for specific symptoms such as nausea or spasticity. These are treated like any other prescription medicine.

In the second, governments run programmes that allow doctors to prescribe herbal cannabis or extracts to qualifying patients, often via pharmacies or licensed producers, with rules on diagnoses, product standards and import. Evidence for many claimed uses remains limited, and regulators differ in how strictly they require proof of benefit. Medical status does not imply that non-medical use is legal, and a medical authorisation usually does not carry over to other countries.

## Driving, workplaces and other secondary rules

Even where possession is lawful, other laws still apply. Driving under the influence is an offence nearly everywhere, and many countries set a limit on THC in blood or saliva or apply zero tolerance. Employers can set drug-free policies in many places, and some professions, such as pilots, drivers and healthcare staff, face special restrictions. Public consumption, consumption near schools, advertising and sales to minors are commonly restricted in regulated markets.

Minimum ages are another common feature: regulated adult-use systems generally set the age at 18 or 21, depending on the jurisdiction. Possession limits, home-grow plant counts and transport rules are specific to each place and can differ between neighbouring regions. These details are the main reason that general summaries, including this one, cannot replace the official text.

## Policy debates and evidence

Debate about policy centres on several questions. Supporters of regulation argue that it removes the illicit market, enables quality control, and frees up police resources, while opponents point to concerns about youth use, high-potency products, commercialisation and public health costs. Decriminalisation advocates emphasise that criminal records for personal possession can be disproportionate to the harm caused.

Research on the effects of legalisation is still developing and results differ between places, so claims that a policy has clearly succeeded or failed should be read critically. Outcomes depend on design details such as taxes, retail density, potency limits and enforcement. For the health background, see the [consumption overview](/en/consumption/) and the research summaries from [NIDA](https://nida.nih.gov/research-topics/cannabis-marijuana) and the [WHO](https://www.who.int/teams/mental-health-and-substance-use/alcohol-drugs-and-addictive-behaviours/drugs-psychoactive/cannabis).

## Why laws change and how to check

Cannabis policy is among the fastest-moving areas of drug law. Governments review thresholds, reclassify substances, adjust possession limits and revise cultivation rules, and courts sometimes strike down parts of the law. Examples above that were true when written may be outdated within a year or two.

Reliable ways to check include the official legal database of your country, the ministry of health or justice, the national drug or medicines agency, and international summaries such as the [European Drug Report pages](https://www.euda.europa.eu/topics/cannabis_en) and [UNODC](https://www.unodc.org/unodc/en/drug-trafficking/cannabis.html). Travellers should know that carrying cannabis across borders is illegal almost everywhere, including between places where it is lawful, and that legal use in one jurisdiction gives no protection in another.
